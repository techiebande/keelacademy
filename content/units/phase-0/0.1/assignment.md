# Write the client brief

Write one page about Lantern Home, in your own words, for someone who has never heard of the shop and knows nothing about computers. Save it as `client-brief.md` in your course folder; you will come back to this document at the end of every phase.

Use these four headings, in this order, because the checks look for them and because this is the order a client expects to read them in:

```text no-run
# Lantern Home: client brief

## The problem
## Who cares, and what done means to each of them
## How a message is handled today
## How a message should be handled
```

Under **The problem**, two to four sentences. What is wrong, how big it is (how many messages, how long the wait), and why it matters to the business. If your friend read only this section, they should understand the situation.

Under **Who cares**, one short paragraph each for Sarah, Michael and Rachel. Name them. Say what each one wants and what would make each one reject the system even if the other two loved it. This is the section most people write too thinly; the chapter's third section is the model.

Under **How a message is handled today**, walk one message through the current process the way the chapter walked Emily Carter's. Numbered steps are fine here, because it really is a sequence. Say where the time goes.

Under **How a message should be handled**, the same walk, but through the system you are going to build. Say what happens to an easy case, what happens to a hard case, and what gets written down. Do not describe how the system works inside; describe what Sarah, Michael and Rachel would see.

Two rules for the words. Do not use "AI", "agent", "LLM", "model" or "machine learning" anywhere in the document. And keep every amount as a plain number like `45.00`, the way Lantern's own records do.

Aim for 350 to 600 words. A brief that runs long is usually one that has started explaining technology.

## How it is checked

Your brief is read by the tutor against a short rubric, and the verdict names the criterion that was not met. There are five:

1. The problem is stated in plain words, with the size of it (how many, how long), and a reader with no background would understand it.
2. All three people are named, and what each one needs is specific to that person, not a general wish for things to be better.
3. The current process is a real sequence a reader could follow, and it says where the time goes.
4. The target process distinguishes easy cases from hard cases and says that decisions are recorded.
5. None of the banned words appear, and the document is within the length.

The verdict comes back within a few minutes. "Not yet" on a criterion is not a failure; it is a note about which section to revisit. Most briefs pass on the second try.

## If you get stuck

### The tutor says the problem section is too general

You have probably written about Lantern the way a brochure would ("Lantern wants to serve its customers better"). Go back to numbers and time: three thousand messages, twenty minutes each, two to three days of waiting, one refund in fifty wrong. A problem you can count is a problem a client will pay to solve.

### The three people sound the same

If your paragraphs for Sarah, Michael and Rachel could be swapped without anyone noticing, they are not yet about those three people. Ask, for each one: what would make this person say no even if everything else worked? Sarah says no to slow. Michael says no to unrecorded. Rachel says no to a system deciding the hard cases alone. Build each paragraph around that.

### A banned word slipped in

Search the document for "AI", "agent", "LLM", "model" and "learning" before you submit. Then ask whether the sentence that contained the word was describing the problem or the solution. It was almost certainly the solution, and the fix is to describe what the person sees instead: "the customer gets an answer within an hour" rather than "an AI agent responds".

### It is much longer than 600 words

You have started explaining how the system works inside. Cut everything after "how it should be handled" that a customer, an accountant or a support lead would not see. What they see is the whole brief.
